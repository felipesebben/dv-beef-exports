# Only Canada freely names its beef importers

*Research date for all access, pricing and legal statements: 2026-10-03. Each claim carries a status: **[verified]** means it was read on a primary or official page, or tested live, on that date. **[snippet]** means it came from a search-result summary or a secondary source. **[inferred]** means it is the researchers' reasoning or background knowledge. Nothing here is legal advice.*

Of the eleven destination markets, **only Canada publishes a free, bulk, product-level list of named importers**. Its Canadian Importers Database (CID) names the firms behind up to 80% of imports of each HS6 or HS10 code, under an open licence. In the **United States (including Puerto Rico)**, ocean-manifest consignee names are public by regulation. They can only be reached free through capped freemium search sites, though, because CBP sells the bulk feed and refused a 2023 FOIA request for it. **Singapore and Paraguay** have partial routes. Singapore's company register is a free bulk download, but nothing in it ties a company to beef. Paraguay has a short list of names from the press, plus a legal right to request the official importer registers. **Mexico, Ghana, Guyana and Libya have no usable free source of names**, and Malaysia has only weak indirect leads. This matters because Mexico is Brazil's fastest-growing market in this scope: about US$497M in 2025, up from US$153M in 2024, per the project's own ComexStat database. The share-of-imports question has a cleaner answer. UN Comtrade covers nine of the ten reporters at HS6 by partner, and its keyless preview endpoint worked live. Libya, whose own reporting stops at 2019, needs mirror data or the open-licensed CEPII BACI. Comtrade's licence restricts raw data to internal use, and **the project's GitHub repo is public** (checked 2026-10-03), so raw Comtrade rows must stay out of the tracked `comexstat.duckdb`. The largest open question is Turkey. Brazil reports shipping it more than US$100M a year of frozen boneless beef. Turkey reports importing about a twentieth of that from Brazil. The research also says Brazilian beef is not yet cleared for import into Turkey at all.

## Canada leads, the US is partial, and four markets publish nothing

In almost every market, the agency that licenses meat imports holds the list we want: Malaysia's DVS e-permits, Singapore's SFA licences, Ghana's VSD permits, Paraguay's SENACSA register, Guyana's GLDA "no objections" and the US FSIS import applications. In almost every market, that list is unpublished. What destinations do publish is lists of approved **foreign plants**. Those name the Brazilian exporters allowed to ship, which maps competitors but not buyers ([FSIS eligible establishments](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/inspection/import-export/import-export-library/eligible-foreign-establishments); [SFA accreditation database](https://www.sfa.gov.sg/tools-and-resources/accreditation-database-for-overseas-sources); [DVS import procedure](https://www.dvs.gov.my/dvs/resources/user_1/2024/BKPBV/IMPORT%20EKSPORT/GENERAL_PROCEDURE_IMPORT_MEAT,_POULTRY,_MILK_AND_EGG_PRODUCTS_TO_MALAYSIA_REV._2_29112024_.pdf)). No target market was found to keep a public register of approved *importers*, the way China's customs agency does. Three patterns stand out. First, **the best free sources are statistical or company registers published for other purposes**, such as Canada's industry-promotion database and Singapore's company register, rather than any veterinary or customs disclosure. Second, the **US is the only market where importer names are public by law**. 19 CFR 103.31 makes inward-manifest consignee data publishable unless the importer certifies confidentiality, and that certification lasts two years and covers vessel manifests only ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/103.31)) [verified]. The free route is still narrow, though. CBP sells AMS data on CD-ROMs ([CBP](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/2024-05/Sea%20AMS%20Data%20Processing%20Services%20May%202024_508.pdf)) [snippet], and it rejected the Data Liberation Project's bulk request the day after it was filed in April 2023 [verified]. Third, in Paraguay, Guyana, Malaysia and the US, **formal information requests are the only free route to the official lists**. They are cheap to file and uncertain to succeed. Paraguay's Ley 5282 is the most promising, because the SENACSA and MIC registers are administrative records rather than tax data, so customs secrecy arguably does not apply [inferred].

Turkey needs separate treatment. The notes describe ESK (Et ve Süt Kurumu) as the sole authorised importer of meat and live cattle since the 2024 reform. A January 2026 opposition bill calls it "the sole authorised state enterprise for meat and live-animal imports" and puts meat imports at 56,240 t in January–November 2025 ([TBMM 2/3448](https://cdn.tbmm.gov.tr/KKBSPublicFile/D28/Y4/T2/WebOnergeMetni/a43cac01-542b-4802-9cc3-59a50eec8df3.pdf)) [verified as the bill's claims]. Halk TV's investigation names ESK's 2023–2025 suppliers, led by Polonia Beef at 47,900 t, and its top domestic buyers, led by Altun Gıda (Happy Center) at 14,100 t. None of the supplier countries is Brazil [verified, secondary press].

## Comtrade answers the market-share question everywhere except Libya

For each destination's **total** imports by supplier, the best free default is the **UN Comtrade API**. The free registered tier gives 500 calls per day at 1 call per second, with up to 100K records per call according to the official page. The `comtradeapicall` README says 250K, so plan around the lower figure. Bulk download is premium-only ([UN Comtrade subscriptions](https://uncomtrade.org/docs/subscriptions/)) [verified]. **The keyless preview endpoint works without registration.** It is capped at 500 records and one period and one product per call. A live call for Ghana's 2024 imports of 0202.30 returned 29 partners, with Brazil at US$6.11M of a US$9.22M total, about 66% ([Comtrade preview](https://comtradeapi.un.org/public/v1/preview/C/A/HS?reporterCode=288&period=2024&cmdCode=020230&flowCode=M)) [verified]. A third call within about three seconds returned HTTP 429 [verified]. Any client should therefore treat 429 as transient, as the ComexStat client already does. The maintained Python client is `comtradeapicall` 1.3.2, MIT-licensed and published by UNSD ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/comtradeapicall/)) [verified].

Mirror data is a usable substitute, with known biases: values are FOB rather than CIF, timing can shift across year-ends, transit shipments distort origin, non-reporting partners drop out, and exporter and importer classifications can differ (India's carabeef under 0202.30, for example) [inferred]. All of these push in the direction of overstating the shares of reporting suppliers such as Brazil. The best cross-check is **CEPII BACI**, release 202601 (2026-01-30), which runs to 2024. It is reconciled for CIF/FOB and reporter reliability, and it is published under the **Etalab 2.0 open licence**, which allows redistribution with attribution ([CEPII](https://www.cepii.fr/CEPII/en/bdd_modele/bdd_modele_item.asp?id=37)) [verified]. WITS re-serves Comtrade under the same restrictions and has a weak HS6-by-partner API ([WITS API](https://wits.worldbank.org/witsapiintro.aspx)) [verified]. ITC Trade Map is free to users in developing countries such as Brazil, but it has no open API and its terms were unreadable ([ITC](https://mas-admintools.intracen.org/accounts/registration.aspx)) [snippet]. It suits manual validation, not a pipeline.

**The licence is the binding constraint for this project.** Comtrade data "are made available for internal use only" and "may not be re-disseminated in any form without prior written consent" of UNSD ([UN Stats wiki](https://unstats.un.org/wiki/display/comtrade/Policy+on+use+and+re-dissemination+of+UN+Comtrade+data)) [verified]. "Transformed or substantially different data" are exempt: new indicators, aggregations and arithmetic can be shared without a fee ([Comtrade FAQ](https://uncomtrade.org/docs/faqs-on-use-and-re-dissemination/)) [verified]. The repo `felipesebben/dv-beef-exports` is **public** (checked with `gh repo view`, 2026-10-03), and ADR 0003 commits `comexstat.duckdb` to git. Pushing raw Comtrade rows into that file would therefore be re-dissemination. The safe pattern is to keep raw and staging Comtrade tables in a gitignored DuckDB file under `data/raw/` and commit only derived marts: Brazil's share, rank and competitor concentration. BACI rows can be committed with attribution. The same split applies to US Census and StatCan data, which are published under open terms [verified for StatCan OGL; Census terms not read].

## Turkey's numbers contradict each other, and the cause is unresolved

The research notes say Brazilian beef **did not have sanitary access to Turkey as of early or mid 2026**. A USDA FAS report described the deal as "in its final negotiation phase" ([USDA GAIN BR2026-0010](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Livestock+and+Products+Semi-annual_Brasilia_Brazil_BR2026-0010.pdf)) [snippet], and a May 2026 op-ed still listed Turkey as "under negotiation" ([Correio Braziliense](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/opiniao/2026/05/7412941-o-jogo-mudou-para-a-carne-bovina-brasileira.html)) [verified]. The notes also describe ESK as effectively the sole importer, with suppliers in the EU and Ukraine. **The project's own data says otherwise.** A query of `marts.exports` in the committed `comexstat.duckdb` (HEAD `ba08097`; the working copy was locked by another process) shows Brazil exporting NCM 0202.30.00 (frozen boneless beef) to Turkey every month for years. Annual totals were **US$23M in 2018, US$82M in 2019, US$57M in 2023, US$124M / 28,068 t in 2024, US$102M / 18,207 t in 2025, and US$82M / 13,508 t in January–August 2026**, plus a growing flow of frozen tongues (0206.21, US$2.2M so far in 2026). The "roughly US$60M a year" figure in the brief matches some years, but 2024–2026 run well above it. The implied price, about US$4.4–5.6/kg, is consistent with frozen boneless beef.

A live Comtrade check run for this report sharpens the gap. **Brazil reports exporting US$124.0M / 28,068 t of 0202.30 to Turkey in 2024** ([Comtrade, Brazil exports](https://comtradeapi.un.org/public/v1/preview/C/A/HS?reporterCode=76&period=2024&cmdCode=020230&flowCode=X&partnerCode=792)) [verified]. **Turkey reports importing only US$5.25M / 858 t of 0202.30 from Brazil**, out of total 0202.30 imports from all origins of just US$19.0M / 1,980 t. New Zealand and Uruguay are its other main origins ([Comtrade, Turkey imports](https://comtradeapi.un.org/public/v1/preview/C/A/HS?reporterCode=792&period=2024&cmdCode=020230&flowCode=M)) [verified]. That is a mirror gap of about 24× in value and 33× in weight. It is far too large to be CIF/FOB or timing noise. It also undercuts both of the simple stories. Turkey does record *some* Brazilian frozen boneless beef entering, so "zero access" is too strong. Yet most of what Brazil declares as bound for Turkey never shows up in Turkey's import statistics.

**This contradiction is unresolved.** The following explanations are all plausible, and none is established:

- **Free-zone or bonded entry.** Goods landed in a Turkish free zone or bonded warehouse may sit outside the trade system Turkey uses for its import statistics. They could still be recorded by Brazil as exports to Turkey. [inferred]
- **Transit or re-export to third countries.** Turkey could be the declared destination for beef actually moving on to other markets, such as Iraq or Syria. Brazilian declarations record the destination known at shipment, and goods in transit never enter Turkish imports. [inferred]
- **Inward processing.** Beef could enter under an inward-processing or temporary-admission regime, either for re-export or for supply to ESK, and be recorded differently on each side. [inferred]
- **Partial or product-specific access.** The research could be wrong about access. Some plants or products may be cleared, which would explain the 858 t Turkey does record, and the notes' "no access" sources may be outdated. The 2010 precedent shows Turkey has opened temporarily before ([Agrolink](https://www.agrolink.com.br/noticias/governo-anuncia-abertura-do-mercado-turco-para-a-carne-brasileira_109886.html)) [verified]. A wholly *recent* opening cannot explain the series, which runs back to at least 2018.
- **Misclassification or miscoding.** On the Brazilian side, the destination country or NCM could be wrong. On the Turkish side, the product could be recorded under another code, such as 0202.20 or 0201. [inferred]
- **Data from before a policy change.** Parts of the series could predate the 2024 ESK reform or a sanitary suspension. This fits poorly, because flows rose after 2024. [inferred]

To verify, the team should take these steps, cheapest first:

1. Pull Comtrade for Turkey as a reporter across all eight HS6 codes for 2018–2025, and compare it with Brazil's reported exports for each year. This shows whether the gap is persistent or limited to some years.
2. Inspect the `customsCode`, `motCode` and `partner2Code` fields in both responses for signs of free-zone or transit coding.
3. Check ComexStat's own breakdown of these shipments by port of dispatch and Brazilian state, if the API exposes them, and look at the monthly pattern.
4. Pull MAPA's SIGSIF list of Brazilian plants approved for Turkey ([MAPA](https://www.gov.br/agricultura/pt-br/guia-de-servicos/estabelecimentos-habilitados-a-exportacao-por-pais)). If no beef plants are listed, the beef is probably not entering Turkey for consumption.
5. Ask the Brazilian agricultural attaché in Ankara, ABIEC or a Brazilian packer directly where the "Turkey" cargo actually ends up.

**Until the gap is resolved, Turkey should be treated as a watch-list market, and its opportunity score in the app as suspect.** The score is computed from Brazil-side data that may not reflect Turkish consumption.

## Brazil-side sources map competitors, and the legal baseline is LGPD plus manual collection

No Brazilian government source names foreign buyers. MAPA's sanitary certificates name consignees but are not published [inferred]. MDIC's old list of Brazilian exporters by destination, which gave value ranges only, appears to have been discontinued on fiscal-secrecy grounds ([comexbrasil.gov.br](http://www.comexbrasil.gov.br/conteudo/ver/chave/empresas)) [snippet]. The most useful Brazil-side free source is **MAPA's SIGSIF list of plants approved to export, by country and product** ([MAPA](https://www.gov.br/agricultura/pt-br/guia-de-servicos/estabelecimentos-habilitados-a-exportacao-por-pais)) [verified]. It shows which competing Brazilian plants can ship to each market. Their foreign customers then become targets for manifest lookups or trade-fair research. The SIGSIF endpoint is an old Oracle gateway, so snapshots should be date-stamped. The most realistic free route to *vetted* buyers is **ApexBrasil's business rounds**. One "Exporta Mais Brasil" food edition brought 34 foreign buyers to meet 103 Brazilian firms ([ApexBrasil](https://apexbrasil.com.br/content/apexbrasil/br/pt/conteudo/noticias/exporta-mais-brasil--edicao-de-alimentos-e-bebidas-saudaveis-dev.html)) [snippet]. ABIEC publishes market totals but no buyer lists ([ABIEC](https://abiec.com.br/beef-report-2026-revela-novo-patamar-da-pecuaria-brasileira/)) [verified as existing]. Trade-fair exhibitor directories are free to browse but limited to personal use. Gulfood, for example, allows copying "for your personal non-commercial use" and forbids republishing ([Gulfood T&C](https://www.gulfood.com/terms-conditions)) [verified].

On personal data, the line that matters is between **company-level records and named people**. LGPD covers processing carried out in Brazil, so it applies to a Brazilian firm holding foreign contacts' data [inferred from art. 3; confirm with counsel]. Legitimate interest is the normal lawful basis for B2B prospecting. ANPD's February 2024 guide requires documented purpose, necessity and balancing tests ([ANPD guide](https://www.gov.br/anpd/pt-br/centrais-de-conteudo/materiais-educativos-e-publicacoes/copy_of_guia_legitimo_interesse.pdf/@@display-file/file)) [snippet]. Small companies may keep a simplified processing record and need no DPO, but must offer a contact channel (Resolução CD/ANPD 2/2022, [LegisWeb](https://www.legisweb.com.br/legislacao/?id=426801)) [snippet]. Destination-side rules vary, all as of 2026-10-03:

- **Singapore** (PDPA s.4(5)) and **Canada** (PIPEDA s.4.01) exclude business contact information. Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) still restricts emailing addresses harvested from directories ([OPC Compu-Finder](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2016/pipeda-2016-003/)) [snippet].
- **California's** B2B exemption expired on 1 Jan 2023, although CCPA thresholds almost certainly exclude a small Brazilian trader ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2023/01/january-1-2023-expiration-of-employee-and-b2b)) [snippet; threshold point inferred].
- **Mexico** replaced its data-protection law (LFPDPPP) on 21 Mar 2025 and abolished INAI ([LFPDPPP](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LFPDPPP.pdf)) [snippet].
- **Turkey's** KVKK amendments took effect on 1 Jun 2024 ([KVKK](https://www.kvkk.gov.tr/Icerik/8142/Kisisel-Verilerin-Yurt-Disina-Aktarilmasi-Rehberi)) [snippet].
- **Malaysia's** PDPA was amended in 2024 ([Raja, Darryl & Loh](https://rajadarrylloh.com/the-personal-data-protection-amendment-act-2024-and-guidelines-on-the-appointment-of-data-protection-officer-and-data-breach-notification/)) [snippet].
- **Ghana's** Act 843 requires controllers to register ([DPC](https://dataprotection.org.gh/registration/)) [snippet].
- Libya, Guyana and Paraguay were not researched.

Whether these destination laws reach a Brazil-based controller is uncertain. The practical answer is to apply the LGPD baseline everywhere: minimal data, stated source, and an honoured opt-out.

On scraping, the governing US precedent is a warning about contracts rather than hacking. In hiQ v. LinkedIn, scraping public pages likely did not violate the anti-hacking law (CFAA), but hiQ ended up liable for **breach of contract** under LinkedIn's ban on scraping. It paid US$500k in damages and had to delete the data ([ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/)) [snippet]. For this project, the order of preference is: official bulk downloads under an open licence (CID, ACRA, StatCan, BACI, DNCP); then manual, low-volume lookups (ImportYeti, SIGSIF, halal directories, chamber directories); then automation only where the terms allow it. **Named contacts must never enter the public repo.** Git history makes an LGPD deletion request (art. 18) a history rewrite. Company-level records belong in tracked marts. People belong in a gitignored file or CRM, alongside a suppression list [inferred best practice].

## Phase 4 should prototype Canada, the US and Singapore, with Paraguay requests in parallel

Free company-level data is good only where it is bulk and official. That points to three prototypes, chosen on data quality and Brazil's 2025 in-scope exports (project ComexStat database). The bar is not commercial priority, which is why Mexico is left out.

| Prototype | Brazil exports, 2025 (in-scope FOB) | Pipeline shape | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Canada** | US$82M (frozen US$60.5M, almost three times 2024; processed US$21.4M) | **Bulk download.** Yearly CID XLSX files (2012–2024) go into `raw.ca_importers`, joined to the CFIA SFC registry CSV and to StatCan CIMT for HS10 volumes. All OGL, so safe for the tracked DB at company level. | The only "good" market. Its multi-year editions show which importers persist. Its first step is a single check: open the HS6 × country file and confirm it lists names per Brazil row. |
| **United States + Puerto Rico** | About US$1.52B US (frozen US$1.09B, processed US$416M) plus US$15M PR | **Hybrid.** Monthly FSIS import CSVs (I-houses, foreign plants, Brazil filter) feed an automated `raw` table. Consignee discovery is **manual**: ImportYeti lookups seeded with the US customers of known Brazilian packers, recorded with source URL and date. A FOIA request to FSIS for importer data from Form 9540-1 is optional. | The biggest flow by far, and importer names are legally public. Expect gaps from opt-outs and air or overland freight. |
| **Singapore** | US$129M (frozen US$120.6M) | **Bulk download plus manual qualification.** Filter the monthly ACRA open-data files by the meat-wholesale SSIC code. Cross-reference SFA's list of accredited Brazilian plants. Qualify by hand. | A free bulk named universe exists, but it needs heavy filtering. First verify the data.gov.sg licence and the SSIC code. |
| *Parallel: Paraguay* | About US$20M in scope (the press puts total Brazilian beef at about US$55M, so most is probably chilled and out of scope [inferred]) | **Formal requests.** File Ley 5282 requests to SENACSA (registered animal-product importers and 2025 certificates by firm) and MIC (beef licencias previas), and add the press seed list. | Costs nothing but a form, with an answer due in 15 business days. If it succeeds, the result is a census of importers. |

The other markets do not justify engineering yet. **Mexico** is the biggest strategic gap: frozen exports grew from US$152M in 2024 to US$496M in 2025, and no free source names its importers. This is the one market where a paid bill-of-lading or pedimento subscription is worth costing out, or where US-manifest data on Mexican consignees and trade-fair work have to carry the load. **Libya** (US$114M frozen) and **Malaysia** (US$67M) are significant flows with no free names, so they stay manual research markets: Libyan company websites, the PTS registry if it becomes public in 2027, JAKIM listings and the Brazilian embassies. **Ghana** stands out for offal, at US$25M and rising, but has no free names. **Guyana** is too small to pursue, at about US$7M. **Turkey** waits on the verification steps above.

Every market also needs the shared **Comtrade share layer**. This means a `raw.partner_imports` append-only table plus a mirror table for Libya, with the same `pull_id`/`fetched_at` provenance and tenacity retry as ComexStat. A `staging` layer should pick reported data over mirror data over BACI, with a `source_type` column. Brazil's share then lands in `marts`. **Raw Comtrade data must live in a gitignored database**, and only derived marts should enter the public repo. That licence decision deserves its own ADR, as does the companion decision to keep named people out of git.

## Conclusion

The practical finding is a split. **Market-share intelligence can be fully automated for free** across all ten destinations, Libya included, if raw Comtrade rows stay private. **Importer identification is a manual, market-by-market craft**, except in Canada. The pipeline shape follows from that: one automated share layer, plus thin per-market importer tables that hold company names, provenance and a confidence flag, never personal contacts. The most valuable future change in data access would be Paraguay's customs portal returning, or any registry that lists importers by product; both are worth monitoring.

The Turkey gap is a warning for the existing app as much as for Phase 4. Brazil-side export statistics record the destination declared when goods ship, not where they are consumed. The opportunity scores built on ComexStat carry that error silently. Cross-checking ComexStat against destination-reported imports for every market, not only Turkey, would be cheap to add with the Comtrade layer. It would turn a one-off anomaly into a standing data-quality check, and it should be built before any market is prioritised on ComexStat growth alone.
